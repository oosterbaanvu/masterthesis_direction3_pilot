# Literature review: stress-testing data-quality controls with error injection and LLM-proposed rules

Draft for an MSc AI thesis at the University of Groningen. Compiled on 27 October 2026.

## How to read this review

This review supports a thesis that builds and studies a "stress tester" for data-quality (DQ) controls. The tester does six things:

1. It injects realistic errors into a dataset.
2. It measures which errors the existing rules catch, as a matrix of error type by control.
3. It lets an LLM propose new rules, as readable code, to close the gaps.
4. It validates those proposals in a sandbox: catch rate, false rejections on clean data and run-to-run stability.
5. A human approves or rejects each proposal.
6. It produces an audit evidence pack.

The core comparison is between four approaches: an LLM that writes rules a human approves ("rule-writer"), an LLM or ML model that labels data directly ("judge"), non-LLM rule suggestion, and expert rule sets. The main case is SEC XBRL data checked by the XBRL US Data Quality Committee (DQC) rules. GLEIF and HMDA data serve as replications.

**Verification.** On 27 October 2026, every source below was opened on its arXiv page, publisher page, ACL Anthology page, an official page, or the PDF itself. Bibliographic details were cross-checked against Crossref where a DOI exists. Each entry carries a status label:

- **[PR]**: peer-reviewed.
- **[PP]**: preprint, not peer-reviewed.
- **[TH]**: thesis.
- **[OFF]**: official, regulatory or standards source.
- **[SW]**: software or documentation.
- **UNVERIFIED**: I could not confirm the source. No details are invented for these.

Where I only saw an abstract or metadata, the entry says so. Figures quoted from papers are the authors' own claims; I did not reproduce them.

**What the closest work does.** LeDQeR is the nearest rule-writer:

- It generates executable Great Expectations rules.
- It filters them for false positives on clean data.
- It removes redundant rules.

Akella et al. is the nearest peer-reviewed counterpart.

**Gap relative to this thesis.**

- No study measures how much **coverage a generated rule adds on top of an existing expert rule set**. LeDQeR frames its work that way in its motivation figure but does not test it.
- Only Alexander et al. measure **run-to-run stability** of generated rule suites, and they do so with human ratings, not set overlap.
- No study evaluates **human approval** as part of the pipeline.
- Nearly all ground truth comes from errors injected into the Raha-era datasets. None uses a regulatory rule set (DQC, GLEIF, HMDA) as the reference.

| "GEIL" | **UNVERIFIED** | Two standard searches and one extended search found no LLM error-detection method by this name. It may be a different acronym or an unpublished work | n/a | n/a |

**Observations.**

- The "judge" category is already **hybrid**. ZeroED and ForestED both have the LLM write executable checks, then use LLM labels or ensembles to decide. The thesis should therefore treat rule-writer versus judge as **positions on a spectrum**, defined by who makes the final per-item decision and whether that decision is a frozen, inspectable artefact.
- Stability is reported unevenly: as a mean only (ZeroED), on two datasets (ForestED), or as a full variance (SEC-FinTables).
- FinVerBench is a useful warning. In most runs, a judge's false-alarm rate on clean financial statements was close to total. This is exactly the failure mode the thesis's sandbox "false rejection" gate is meant to catch.
- **Gap:** no judge study reports reviewer effort, auditability, or a comparison with an expert rule set on the same errors.

**Gap.** Error generators are used to benchmark cleaning algorithms or ML robustness, **not to assess the adequacy of a deployed rule set**. Their operators are also generic: typos, missing values, swaps, noise. The XBRL case needs **domain mutation operators** that no generic library provides, such as sign flips, scale (×1000) errors, wrong period or context, wrong concept, and dimension/member misuse. Those operators would be part of the thesis's contribution.

## 5. Novelty check: mutation testing applied to data validation

**The software-engineering basis is well established:**

- Jia & Harman, *An Analysis and Survey of the Development of Mutation Testing*, IEEE TSE 37(5):649–678 (2011), doi:10.1109/TSE.2010.62 [PR].
- Just et al., *Are Mutants a Valid Substitute for Real Faults in Software Testing?*, FSE 2014, pp. 654–665, doi:10.1145/2635868.2635929 [PR]. Detecting mutants correlates with detecting real faults.
- Papadakis, Shin, Yoo, Bae, *Are Mutation Scores Correlated with Real Fault Detection?*, ICSE 2018, pp. 537–548, doi:10.1145/3180155.3180183 [PR]. Once test-suite size is controlled for, the correlation is weak.

For the thesis, this means **kill rates on synthetic errors must be validated against natural errors**. The 10-K/A design does exactly that.

**Prior work closest to "mutation analysis of data-quality checks":**

| Work | What it does | How close |
|---|---|---|
| Homayouni, Ghosh, Ray, *An Approach for Testing the Extract-Transform-Load Process in Data Warehouse Systems*, IDEAS 2018, pp. 236–245, doi:10.1145/3216122.3216149 [PR]; also Homayouni MSc thesis, Colorado State 2017 [TH] | Mutation operators applied to **data in the target warehouse** measure the fault-finding ability of generated ETL "balancing" assertions. 92% of 118 seeded faults were detected. The thesis states that DQ approaches lack "well-defined test adequacy criteria" and recommends **mutation analysis to evaluate data-quality tests** | **Very close in concept.** Differences: one generated test suite, mock data (100 records per table), generic operators, no per-control matrix over an expert rule set, no natural errors, no gap-closing |
| Homayouni, *Anomaly Detection and Explanation in Big Data*, PhD dissertation, Colorado State 2021 [TH] | Uses a **"mutation score"** with data mutation operators to evaluate learned constraint and anomaly detectors (IDEAL/ADQuaTe) | Close in method. The object under test is a learned detector, not a rule set |
| Schelter, Rukat, Biessmann, *JENGA*, EDBT 2021 [PR], `SchemaStresstest` | Applies 250 random corruptions to serving data and checks whether a **TFDV schema** flags each one. A corruption counts as a true or false positive or negative depending on whether model AUC drops more than 3%. False negatives lead to concrete schema fixes (a range check; length and encoding checks). The paper says "data scientists can iteratively improve their integrity constraints until they pass the stresstest" | **The closest precursor to steps 1–3.** Differences: the oracle is model impact, not error presence; one dataset and one schema; random corruptions; gaps are closed by hand |
| McMinn, Wright, Kapfhammer, *The Effectiveness of Test Coverage Criteria for Relational Database Schema Integrity Constraints*, ACM TOSEM 25(1), 2015 [PR] | Mutates **integrity constraints** to evaluate test data. This is the inverse direction | Related, but inverted |
| Viganò, Cornejo, Pastore, Briand, *Data-Driven Mutation Analysis for Cyber-Physical Systems* (DaMAT), IEEE TSE 49(4):2182–2201 (2023), doi:10.1109/TSE.2022.3213041 [PR] | Mutates data exchanged between components, using tabular fault models configured by engineers, to assess test suites | Same idea in a different domain. The "fault-model table" maps well onto an error-type taxonomy |
| Gargouri & Reza, arXiv 2605.20500 [PP] | Anomaly injection to compare manual and LLM-augmented dbt tests | Small-scale (16 anomalies), and the paper does not call it mutation analysis |
| Yao, Nimje, Viswanath, Dutta, *NBTestGen*, arXiv 2509.13656 v2 [PP] | LLM-generated assertions for ML notebooks kill 72.21% of ML-specific mutations | LLM-written checks evaluated by mutation, but on notebooks, not data rule sets |

**Verdict.** Applying mutation analysis or fault injection to data-quality checks is **not new**. Homayouni and colleagues proposed and ran it in 2017–2018. JENGA operationalised it for ML data-validation schemas in 2021. I found **no** work that does any of the following:

- (a) builds a **per-rule × per-error-type kill matrix over a large, published, expert rule set** (DQC has about 200 rules);
- (b) **validates synthetic mutant kill rates against natural errors** in data, in the way Just et al. did for code;
- (c) uses **surviving mutants as the specification for LLM rule synthesis**, with acceptance gated on sandbox metrics.

Searches covered "mutation testing data validation", "test adequacy data quality checks", "data quality test coverage", "fault injection data pipelines validation coverage" and variants. Grey literature from proprietary DQ vendors was not systematically covered and remains a residual risk.

## 6. Natural error benchmarks and correction histories

- **Abedjan et al.**, *Detecting Data Errors: Where Are We and What Needs to Be Done?*, PVLDB 9(12):993–1004 (2016), doi:10.14778/2994509.2994518 [PR]. Five real datasets with ground truth. Every tool's coverage was "well below 100%".
- **Raha** (Mahdavi et al., SIGMOD 2019, pp. 865–882, doi:10.1145/3299869.3324956) and **Baran** (Mahdavi & Abedjan, PVLDB 13(12):1948–1961, 2020, doi:10.14778/3407790.3407801) [PR]. These supply the standard Hospital, Flights, Beers, Rayyan and Movies collection used by ZeroED, ForestED, LeDQeR and others. Baran pre-trains correctors on Wikipedia revision histories.
- **Hasan & Mahdavi**, *Automatic Error Correction Using the Wikipedia Page Revision History*, CIKM 2021, pp. 3073–3077, doi:10.1145/3459637.3482062 [PR]. Revision logs used as a source of natural corrections.
- **Auto-Detect** Wikipedia-table benchmark (§3) and **TableEG**'s 12 real datasets (§4).
- **Brown, Crowley, Elliott**, *What Are You Saying? Using Topic to Detect Financial Misreporting*, J. Accounting Research 58(1):237–291 (2020), doi:10.1111/1475-679X.12294 [PR]. Misreporting labels come from AAERs and from "irregularities identified from financial restatements and 10-K filing amendments". This is a precedent for **amendments as labels**, but at firm-year level and with text features, not cell-level XBRL facts.
- **FinAuditing** (§2) uses real DQC messages as labels. **AuditFraudBench** (Liu et al., arXiv 2606.08345 [PP], "work in progress") uses original and restated 10-K/10-Q filings for fraud tasks. The abstract does not mention XBRL alignment.

**Gap.** I found **no cell-level benchmark of XBRL data errors derived from original versus amended filings**. Three caveats must be designed for:

- Amendments contain many changes that are not error corrections, such as Part III information, exhibits and auditor consents. A diff is therefore not an error label without a filtering protocol.
- Amended errors are a selection: errors that *someone noticed*.
- Some amendments are likely *triggered by DQC or EDGAR validation*. This creates **circularity**: DQC will trivially "catch" errors that were corrected because DQC flagged them. A defensible design stratifies or excludes those cases, for example by checking whether the original filing already violated a DQC rule.

## 7. XBRL and financial-reporting data quality

- **Debreceny, Farewell, Piechocki, Felden, Gräning**, *Does It Add Up?*, J. Accounting and Public Policy 29(3):296–306 (2010), doi:10.1016/j.jaccpubpol.2010.04.001 [PR]. About a quarter of the first filings by 400 large companies had calculation inconsistencies. The main cause was mishandled debit/credit assumptions.
- **Du, Vasarhelyi, Zheng**, *XBRL Mandate: Thousands of Filing Errors and So What?*, J. Information Systems 27(1):61–78 (2013), doi:10.2308/isys-50399 [PR]. Metadata verified only; content not read.
- **XBRL US DQC rules** [OFF/SW]:
  - xbrl.us/data-quality/rules-guidance lists "202 DQC Approved Rules", freely available, with plugin version 31.0.2 dated October 2026.
  - GitHub DataQualityCommittee/dqc_us_rules holds the rules written in **Xule** and run as an Arelle plugin, with a test suite. Licence terms are at xbrl.us/dqc-license; I did not read them, so check before redistributing.
  - XBRL US reports a 64% error reduction from Q1 2015 to Q1 2016 for data covered by the first rule set. This is self-reported and not peer-reviewed.
- **Foshag**, *The Capital Market Effects of Machine-Readable Data Errors: Evidence from XBRL US DQC Rules*, PhD dissertation, Univ. of Tennessee 2025 [TH]. Uses DQC errors in 10-Ks as the quality measure and finds **no evidence** of capital-market effects.
- **SEC Financial Statement Data Sets** [OFF]: covers January 2009 to September 2026, updated quarterly. The SEC states it "cannot guarantee the accuracy of the data sets".
- LLM benchmarks on XBRL: SEC-FinTables, FinAuditing, FinVerBench and FinRule-Bench (§2).

**Gap.** Accounting research uses DQC flags as an *outcome or measure*. LLM benchmarks use them as *labels*. **Nobody I found evaluates the DQC rule set itself**: which error types it misses, what it costs in false alarms, and how an LLM or profiler could extend it. This is a genuine niche.

## 8. AI governance, data quality standards and LLM non-determinism

**EU AI Act, Article 10** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) [OFF]:

- 10(2) requires data-governance practices covering, among other things, (c) data preparation "such as annotation, labelling, cleaning…" and (h) identifying "data gaps or shortcomings" and how to address them.
- 10(3) requires datasets to be "to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete".

I checked this text through the unofficial consolidation at artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/10. The EUR-Lex fetch failed, so **verify the wording against the Official Journal**. That site marks Article 10 as amended, apparently by the "Digital Omnibus on AI". A secondary legal source (Legal500/Mondaq) gives it as **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**, published in the OJ on 24 July 2026. It moves Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and Annex I obligations to 2 August 2028. **I did not verify this against the OJ.**

Relevance caveat: the stress tester is not itself a high-risk AI system. Its relevance is as *evidence* for Article 10 obligations on downstream high-risk systems, for example in credit (not re-verified here). Legal interpretation should be checked by the bank's compliance function.

**Operationalisation of the AI Act**

- Buscemi, Deckenbrunnen, Kabir, Mishchenko, Mowla, *Assessing High-Risk AI Systems under the EU AI Act: From Legal Requirements to Technical Verification*, arXiv 2512.13907 v3 [PP]. Maps AI Act requirements to verification activities by type and lifecycle stage. It is generic and does not specify DQ-control evidence.

**Standards** [OFF; only metadata seen, full texts are paywalled]

- **ISO/IEC 5259-1:2024**: Data quality for analytics and ML, Part 1, overview, terminology and examples. Parts 2–5 cover DQ measures, management requirements, a process framework and a governance framework, published 2024–2025.
- **ISO/IEC 25012:2008**: data-quality model with 15 characteristics, seen from an "inherent" and a "system-dependent" point of view.
- **GLEIF Data Quality Checks**: each check has a maturity level, a quality criterion (12 criteria, from accuracy to validity) and a formal "if precondition then condition" specification. They are published as a "Data Quality Rule Setting" (v3.5 at the time of access). The rule-set form fits a coverage matrix well.
- **HMDA 2024 Filing Instructions Guide** (FFIEC/CFPB) defines four edit types: *syntactical* and *validity* edits block submission, while *quality* and *macro-quality* edits require the filer to confirm or correct.
  - **Caveat**: public HMDA data are a privacy-modified loan/application register (LAR), so not every edit can be evaluated on public data.

**Banking context** [OFF]

- BCBS 239, *Principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting*, published 9 January 2013 (bis.org page verified; the PDF could not be fetched).
- ECB *Guide on effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting*, May 2024 (verified through secondary sources only). Among its focus areas is "the effectiveness of data quality controls", which is a direct hook for a control stress test.

**Audit and human oversight** [PR]

- Raji et al., *Closing the AI Accountability Gap*, FAT* 2020, pp. 33–44, doi:10.1145/3351095.3372873. An internal audit framework built on documentation artefacts.
- Sterz et al., *On the Quest for Effectiveness in Human Oversight*, FAccT 2024, pp. 2495–2507, doi:10.1145/3630106.3659051. Effective oversight needs causal power, epistemic access, self-control and fitting intentions.
- Parasuraman, Sheridan, Wickens, IEEE SMC-A 30(3):286–297 (2000), doi:10.1109/3468.844354. Four automation functions (acquisition, analysis, decision, action) at graded levels. This maps directly onto detect / explain / recommend / approve / auto-change.
- Parasuraman & Manzey, *Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation*, Human Factors 52(3):381–410 (2010), doi:10.1177/0018720810376055. Automation bias causes omission and commission errors. This is a risk for the "human approves" step.

**LLM non-determinism**

- Atil et al., *Non-Determinism of "Deterministic" LLM Settings*, arXiv 2408.04667 v5 [PP]. Accuracy varies by up to 15% across runs. Proposes the agreement metrics TARr@N and TARa@N.
- Ouyang, Zhang, Harman, Wang, ACM TOSEM 34(2), 2025, doi:10.1145/3697010 [PR]. Temperature 0 does not guarantee deterministic code generation.
- He / Thinking Machines, *Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference*, blog, 10 September 2025 (not peer-reviewed). Lack of batch invariance in inference kernels causes the variation: 80 unique completions in 1,000 at temperature 0, all identical with batch-invariant kernels.

**Implication.** The rule-writer design does not make *generation* reproducible. It makes the *deployed artefact* frozen, hashable and re-executable. Stability can then be measured as agreement between accepted rule sets across N generations, a TAR-style metric. This is the strongest governance argument for the design, but it is an argument, not yet an empirical finding.

## 9. Human review cost, alert fatigue and review budgets

- **Abedjan et al. (2016)** give an explicit cost model. A tool is worth running only if precision P/(P+N) exceeds C/V, the cost of a human check divided by the value of a found error. They note that enterprises "impose a human budget", and they order tools so that manual verification falls.
- **TFDV (Breck et al. 2019)**: spammy alerts get ignored.
- **Shankar, Garcia, Hellerstein, Parameswaran**, Proc. ACM HCI 8(CSCW1), Art. 206 (2024), doi:10.1145/3653697 (preprint arXiv 2209.09125). Interviewees describe "alert fatigue". One says "90% of them aren't immediate".
- **Raha** budgets about 20 labelled tuples. **AVH** offers precision guarantees. **Deequ** treats suggestion as a human-in-the-loop step.
- Oettershagen, *Top-k on a Budget: Adaptive Ranking with Weak and Strong Oracles*, arXiv 2601.20989 [PP]. A formal model with a cheap noisy oracle and a scarce expert oracle.

**Gap.** DQ papers report F1, not **precision at a fixed review budget** or **false alarms per 10,000 clean records**. Abedjan's C/V threshold is a ready-made, citable basis for a budget-aware metric in the thesis.

## 10. Novelty assessment

**Already done (cite, do not claim):**

- Injecting errors to check whether validation rules catch them: Homayouni et al. 2017/2018/2021, and JENGA `SchemaStresstest` 2021, including fixing schemas from false negatives.
- LLMs writing executable DQ rules or validators: LeDQeR, Akella et al., LLM-DQR, Alexander et al., Quality by Prompt, Cocoon, GIDCL. ZeroED's and ForestED's code criteria also fall here.
- Filtering generated rules for false positives on clean data: LeDQeR. Precision-guaranteed constraint programming: AVH.
- LLM-as-judge on SEC/XBRL tables: SEC-FinTables, FinVerBench, FinAuditing (real DQC labels).
- Realistic error generation: tab_err/JDIQ, TableEG, BART, SAVAGE. SAVAGE already uses the "stress-testing" label.

**Appears new (not found after targeted searching):**

- A per-rule × per-error-type coverage (kill) matrix for a large public expert rule set (DQC), replicated on GLEIF and HMDA rule sets.
- Validating synthetic kill rates against natural cell-level errors mined from original versus amended XBRL filings.
- A head-to-head comparison of rule-writer, judge, profiling-based suggestion and expert rules on the same errors, using catch rate, false rejection on clean data, run-to-run stability and reviewer effort at a fixed budget.
- An audit evidence pack tied to Article 10(2)(h)/(3) and to the effectiveness of bank DQ controls.

**Uncertain:**

- Proprietary DQ vendors may already offer mutation-style rule testing. This grey literature was not reviewed.
- 10-K/A diffs may not yield enough clean, non-circular, cell-level errors.
- The rule-writer/judge distinction may blur, since ZeroED and ForestED are hybrids.
- LLMs may write good DQC-style checks (in Xule) or only pandas-level checks. The rule language affects comparability with the expert set.
- The legal timing of the AI Act after the Digital Omnibus is unverified against the OJ.

**Candidate contribution statements**

1. **C1. Coverage adequacy of a regulatory DQ rule set.** "We adapt mutation analysis to regulatory data-quality rule sets and report a per-rule, per-error-type kill matrix for the XBRL US DQC rules on SEC filings, replicated on GLEIF and HMDA rule sets."
   - *Evidence:* the precursors (Homayouni; JENGA) covered a single suite, used a model-impact oracle and small or mock data. FinAuditing uses DQC messages as labels, not as a coverage object. No DQC coverage study was found.
   - *Risk:* a "first" claim must be hedged, and the precursors must be cited prominently.

2. **C2. Mutant realism for data checks.** "We test whether kill rates on injected errors predict catch rates on natural errors derived from amended XBRL filings."
   - *Evidence:* Just et al. and Papadakis et al. did this for code. tab_err and TableEG validate realism on Raha-type data, not regulatory rule sets. No XBRL amendment-based cell-level error set was found.
   - *Risk:* label quality and circularity (§6). This may become a negative or partial result, which is still publishable if well designed.

3. **C3. Rule-writer versus judge under audit constraints.** "On identical errors, we compare LLM-written approved rules, LLM/ML judges (ZeroED/ForestED-style), profiling-based suggestions (Deequ/TFDV-style) and expert rules on catch rate, false rejection, run-to-run stability and reviewer effort at a fixed budget."
   - *Evidence:* stability is missing (LeDQeR, Akella), mean-only (ZeroED), limited to two datasets (ForestED) or human-rated (Alexander). FinVerBench shows extreme false-alarm variation. No study uses review budgets.
   - *Risk:* compute cost of the judge baselines, and fairness of the comparison when the rule-writer gets more human input.

4. **C4. Gap-directed rule synthesis.** "Surviving mutants serve as counterexamples that drive LLM rule proposals, which are accepted only through sandbox gates and human approval."
   - *Evidence:* LeDQeR generates from single erroneous records. Zhao et al. revise against counterexamples for synthetic data. JENGA closes gaps by hand.
   - *Risk:* incremental novelty. It is strongest if combined with C1/C3.

5. **C5. Evidence pack for AI-Act and BCBS 239-style control assurance.** "We specify and pilot an audit evidence format that links stress-test results to Article 10(2)(h)/(3) and to DQ-control effectiveness, with explicit levels of automation."
   - *Evidence:* Buscemi et al. map requirements generically. Raji et al. supply audit artefacts. No DQ-control-specific format was found.
   - *Risk:* weaker scientific novelty, and it needs expert (auditor) feedback to evaluate.

## 11. Recommended must-reads

1. **Schelter, Rukat, Biessmann, JENGA (EDBT 2021).** The `SchemaStresstest` is the closest precursor to the stress tester. Read Section 4.2.
2. **Homayouni, Ghosh, Ray (IDEAS 2018), plus the 2017 MSc thesis, ch. 2 and 6.** Explicit mutation analysis of data-quality tests and test-adequacy criteria.
3. **Glock et al., LeDQeR (arXiv 2609.06053).** The nearest LLM rule-writer, with Great Expectations output and false-positive filters. Your direct competitor and baseline.
4. **Ni et al., ZeroED (ICDE 2025) and Wang et al., ForestED (KDD 2026).** The state-of-the-art judges, and their hybrid use of LLM-written code.
5. **Abedjan et al. (PVLDB 2016).** Coverage below 100% and the human-cost model behind a budget metric.
6. **Jung et al., tab_err (JDIQ 2025).** Error mechanism versus error type, for realistic injection.
7. **Schelter et al., Deequ (PVLDB 2018), and Martin et al. (PVLDB 2025).** The non-LLM suggestion baseline, and why discovered constraints are mostly false.
8. **Just et al. (FSE 2014) and Papadakis et al. (ICSE 2018).** How to argue, and test, that mutants stand in for real faults.
9. **Ke et al., SEC-FinTables (Findings ACL 2026), and Wang et al., FinAuditing (SIGIR 2026).** The XBRL LLM benchmarks you will be compared against. FinAuditing shows how to harvest DQC messages.
10. **Zhu et al., SAVAGE (PVLDB 2025).** The critique of simplistic corruption and the regulatory "stress-testing" framing. Position against it explicitly.
